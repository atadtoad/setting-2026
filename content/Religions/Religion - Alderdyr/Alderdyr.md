## Summary

Alderdyr has no unified doctrine. Each village interprets its local Alderbeast differently, developing its own traditions, taboos, and rituals over generations. There is no central authority, no holy text, and no formal priesthood.

Followers do not pray to Alderbeasts for salvation or guidance in the afterlife. Alderbeasts are petitioned for worldly matters: protection from raiders, bountiful harvests, safe travel through dangerous lands, mild winters, and healthy livestock. The faith is practical, rooted in survival.

At its heart lies the belief that the land itself is alive. The forests, mountains, rivers, and fields possess a will of their own. Alderbeasts are the voice of that will, guardians who embody the spirit of their territory. To honor them is to honor the land. To defy them is to invite ruin.

The Colossi are viewed separately from Alderbeasts. They are not guardians but disasters, monstrous forces of destruction no different from earthquakes or floods.

## The Wisewomen and Cunningfolk

Those who tend to Alderbeast shrines and interpret their will are not priests in any formal sense. They are intermediaries between the divine and the mortal. They go by many names depending on region: Wisewomen, Cunningmen, druids, witches, wild mages, herbalists, or alchemists.

They read bones, entrails, weather patterns, and animal behavior to discern an Alderbeast's will. Their knowledge is passed down orally through apprenticeship. Some villages maintain both a Wisewoman following the female tradition and a Cunningman following the male tradition. Others have only one or the other.

## Shrines and Sacred Sites

Shrines take the form of stone statues depicting the local Alderbeast, placed at forest edges, crossroads, and mountain passes. Some shrines are ancient, predating the villages that now tend them, inherited from forgotten peoples who once walked these lands.

Larger Alderbeasts may have multiple shrines scattered across their territory. Defiling a shrine is considered an invitation for the Alderbeast's wrath to fall upon the entire region it protects.

## Taboos and Offerings

Each Alderbeast demands different things of those who dwell within its domain. One may forbid the cutting of certain trees. Another may demand silence after dark. Offerings vary as well: some ask for fruits, others for wood, some for blood, and others for a life.

When a Magbeast begins protecting an area consistently, villagers start leaving offerings. If the creature responds through protection, blessings, or omens, a shrine is built. The local Wisewoman formally names the Alderbeast and establishes its customs. This process can take generations.

## Relationship with the Myrkvargr Covenant

The Myrkvargr Covenant worships one specific Alderbeast: the Black Wolf of Lorden. While Alderdyr is the broader, decentralized folk faith, the Myrkvargr Covenant is a more organized tradition within it.

Some tension exists between followers of the Covenant who view the Black Wolf as supreme among Alderbeasts and those who honor their local guardians as equals to any other.

## Relationship with Other Faiths

Ravanians view Alderdyr as primitive superstition, further proof of Lorden's barbarism and need for civilizing conquest.

The theocracy of Aldergard tolerates it as a lesser concern compared to greater threats.

The Arcanists of Lorden officially dismiss it as folk nonsense. Many privately leave offerings before dangerous work.
