## Astravia - The Kingdom of Gold

### Origins and Culture

Astravia, called the Aurum Reich in its own tongue, was once among the largest kingdoms of the eastern world. Its territories stretched across vast mountain ranges and fertile valleys far to the southwest of the Argyros peninsula where Greschia and Veistyria stood. Its people, the Ravii, traced their ancestry to the same underground kingdom that spawned the Veistri during the Age of Grays. When humanity dwelt beneath the stone, the cavern networks stretched vast and far. The ancestors of both peoples emerged from passages at opposite ends of the same great warren, separated by distance but united by blood remembered in old songs and carved lineages.

While the Veistri became warriors and conquerors on the Valegren plateaus, the Ravii settled in lands blessed with abundant gold deposits. They became miners, metalworkers, and merchants rather than soldiers. Gold shaped everything about Astravian society. Their temples gleamed with gilded altars. Their monarchs wore crowns of solid gold rather than iron or steel. Their priests taught that gold carried sacred essence, a remnant of divine light that had guided their ancestors through the dark underground passages toward the surface. To give gold in offering was to share in the divine. To hoard it was to invite misfortune.

#### Astravian Gold

The mines of the Aurum Reich produced gold of legendary purity. Where other kingdoms struggled to refine gold free of copper, silver, or baser metals, Astravian smiths had perfected techniques passed down through generations that yielded metal of near-perfect quality. This purity was not merely aesthetic. Astravian priests had long observed that their purest gold behaved differently in sacred rites, that it held blessing longer and seemed to resonate with divine presence more strongly than gold from foreign mines.

The phrase "Astravian gold" became synonymous across the known world with gold of the highest purity. Merchants in distant lands used it as a standard of measurement. Alchemists sought Astravian techniques. Smiths who could replicate its quality commanded fortunes. Even after Astravia itself was absorbed into the Ravanian Empire, the term persisted. Modern Ravanians still speak of "Astravian gold" when they mean gold pure enough for sacred use, pure enough to be imbued into living flesh without the slow poisoning that impure gold causes over decades.

#### The Aurian Priesthood

The Ravii worshipped a pantheon that shared distant roots with the Veistyrian gods, both traditions descended from beliefs formed in the underground darkness. Their chief deity was a solar figure associated with gold, light, and the sacred fire that had guided humanity from below. Their priesthood, known as the Aurian Priests, served as keepers of the temple flames and interpreters of divine will. Unlike many traditions, the Aurian Priesthood accepted both men and women in equal standing, though women tended toward the ritual and healing arts while men often served as temple guardians and administrators.

These priests held considerable influence in Astravian society, advising monarchs and blessing important ventures. They maintained the sacred techniques for refining Astravian gold to its legendary purity, guarding these methods as religious mysteries. They also developed practices that would later prove fateful: the integration of gold into living flesh.

#### The Golden Limbs

Among the Ravii, those who lost limbs to accident, war, or disease did not simply accept wooden substitutes or live as cripples. The wealthiest commissioned prosthetics of pure gold, crafted by master smiths who specialized in nothing else. A golden hand could not grip as flesh did, but it served as both functional tool and statement of status. A golden leg, properly balanced, allowed its wearer to walk with dignity.

This tradition was more than vanity. The Aurian Priests taught that gold, being sacred, could serve where flesh failed. A man with a golden arm remained whole in the eyes of the gods. The metal replaced what was lost not merely physically but spiritually. Some priests claimed that those with golden limbs could feel through them, that the sacred metal carried sensation where base wood or iron could not.

The wealthiest Astravian families maintained smiths who crafted nothing but golden prosthetics, each piece tailored to its wearer with joints that moved through clever engineering and surfaces etched with prayers. This practice extended beyond mere limbs. The Aurian Priests developed techniques to replace damaged organs with golden substitutes, to reinforce weakened bones with gold threading, even to infuse gold dust into the blood itself for certain sacred purposes.

#### Architecture and Grandeur

At its height, Astravia was a kingdom of breathtaking scale. Its architecture favored the ornate and the vertical, stone facades carved with intricate tracery and topped with gilded spires that caught the mountain sun. Entire districts were constructed from gold-veined marble, the metal running through the stone in natural seams that the Ravii builders incorporated into their designs. Temple complexes sprawled across mountainsides, their golden domes visible for miles. The royal palace at the capital was said to contain more gold than some kingdoms possessed in their entire treasuries.

The Ravii dressed in layered robes of fine cloth, the hems weighted with golden thread, their fingers heavy with rings. They were not warriors, but they were proud, and they believed their gold-blessed civilization represented the highest achievement of humanity. Their court culture emphasized elaborate ceremony, precise etiquette, and displays of wealth that bordered on the devotional.

### The Rostrot

Astravia's decline began not with invasion but with plague.

The sickness appeared first in the mining settlements of the eastern mountains, among workers who delved deep for gold. It spread slowly at first, easily mistaken for common illness. Fever. Weakness. A pallid cast to the skin. But within weeks, the true horror revealed itself.

Those afflicted did not simply die. Their blood thickened, darkening to a rust-brown color visible through increasingly translucent skin. Their movements slowed, becoming sluggish and mechanical. Their minds faded, leaving behind only base impulses: hunger, aggression, a terrible persistence. The infected lost all recognition of family, friends, or self. They became hollow things wearing familiar faces, shambling through villages they had once called home, attacking anything that moved with a lethargy that belied their terrible resilience.

Wounds that would have felled a healthy man barely slowed the afflicted. Limbs could be severed and they would continue crawling forward. Only destruction of the head or complete dismemberment stopped them permanently. And worse, their bites and scratches spread the plague. A single infected villager could doom an entire settlement within a month.

The Aurian Priests named it the Rostrot, for the rust-colored corruption that consumed the victims' blood. They sought desperately for cures. Prayer failed. Conventional medicine failed. In desperation, they turned to the one substance they believed carried divine protection: gold.

#### Gold Against the Plague

The priests discovered that gold could slow the Rostrot's progression. When they replaced infected tissue with golden substitutes, the spread halted at the boundary of sacred metal. A man whose arm was infected could survive if the arm was removed and replaced with gold before the sickness reached his torso. The gold did not cure the plague, but it contained it, walling off the corruption behind barriers of purified metal.

This discovery led to increasingly desperate measures. The Aurian Priests began replacing not just limbs but organs, sections of skin, even portions of bone. Those with wealth enough to afford the procedures became amalgamations of flesh and gold, more metal than man in some cases. They lived, after a fashion. They retained their minds, unlike the fully infected. But they were changed, their movements stiff, their bodies heavy with sacred metal that had saved them at the cost of what they once were.

Not all procedures succeeded. Some patients rejected the gold, their bodies fighting the sacred metal as though it were poison. Others survived the surgery only to succumb to infection, their gold-threaded corpses rising as something worse than ordinary plagued. The Ravii called these horrors Aurimänner, the Gold-Men. They shambled through the abandoned eastern provinces, their golden components gleaming beneath layers of rust and dried blood, their partially gilded forms frozen in postures of eternal hunger. The gold that had been meant to save them now made them far more dangerous than common infected, granting them strength and durability beyond what rotting flesh should possess.

Entire mining districts had to be sealed off, their populations written off as lost, the roads blocked with stone and prayer to contain what lurked within.

### A Kingdom Crumbling

The Rostrot could not be stopped. It could only be contained, and containment meant abandonment. Generation after generation, the Ravii retreated westward, sealing off province after province, leaving behind the empty shells of once-thriving cities. The golden spires still stood, but they rose now over streets where no living foot had tread in decades. Temple complexes that had once housed thousands of priests fell silent, their sacred flames extinguished, their gilded halls home only to dust and the occasional wandering Aurimänner.

By the time King Albrecht III inherited the throne, Astravia had lost more than half its former territory to the quarantine zones. The population that had once numbered in the millions had dwindled to hundreds of thousands, concentrated in the western heartland behind walls patrolled day and night for any sign of infection. The treasury, once overflowing, now struggled to maintain the barriers that kept the Rostrot at bay.

The villages that remained near the quarantine boundaries existed in a state of perpetual dread. Their inhabitants lived in near-empty settlements, entire streets abandoned as families fled westward toward the capital. Those who stayed did so because they had nowhere else to go, or because someone had to maintain the warning fires that signaled when the dead began to walk again. These frontier Ravii grew gaunt and hollow-eyed, jumping at shadows, checking their neighbors' skin for the telltale pallor that signaled infection.

The wild tribes of what would become Eldvatr sensed Astravian weakness. They pressed against the western marches, raiding the outlying settlements, ambushing trade caravans. The Ravii had never been warriors. Their mercenary armies had scattered during the chaos of the Bounded Wars, and Astravian gold could no longer buy protection that did not exist. Each year brought new losses. Each year the kingdom shrank further.

King Albrecht watched his realm die by inches. The great structures his ancestors had built stood empty, monuments to a glory that would never return. The golden domes still caught the sun, but they gleamed now over cities of ghosts. His advisors spoke of alliance through marriage, of finding some distant kingdom whose armies might be pledged through union with the Astravian royal house. But what kingdom would bind itself to a realm so clearly cursed?

### The Arrival of the Refugees

In the final years of the Third Era, around Year 3116, scouts reported a vast host approaching from the east. Albrecht prepared for the end. His remaining soldiers, barely enough to garrison the capital, could not hope to repel an invasion. He ordered the treasury secured and the royal family made ready for flight, though he knew there was nowhere left to flee.

But these were no invaders. They were the shattered remnants of two kingdoms: Veistri soldiers still bearing arms stained with demon-blood from their running battles against Pyrgon's horrors, Greschian sorcerers clutching grimoires that contained knowledge of power beyond anything Astravia possessed, and countless common folk who had fled the Colossi's devastation. They had first fled to Greschia after Valk's destruction, but another Colossus had been detected approaching from the east. They could not remain. They had marched southwest, seeking any land that might shelter them.

At their head rode King Margric Artas Ordos I, a man of both Veistri and Greschian blood, whose very existence bridged the two fallen kingdoms. He sought sanctuary for his people, and he brought something Astravia desperately needed: an army hardened by years of war, sorcerers whose blood-magic could shatter enemies both living and dead, and administrative expertise from governing two kingdoms.

### The Greschian Sorcerers and the Aurimänner

The Greschian sorcerers who accompanied Margric had long known that gold stabilized and amplified their magic. In Greschia, they had used golden implements, golden jewelry, golden dust mixed into their ritual inks. Without gold, the chaotic energies of Gheliach writhed and shifted, refusing to hold shape, slipping through a practitioner's grasp no matter their skill. With gold, the power became cooperative, controllable, vastly more potent.

But Greschian gold had never been pure enough for true imbuing. The metal they possessed, traded from distant lands or mined from shallow deposits, contained impurities that would slowly poison anyone who integrated it into their flesh. They used gold externally, in jewelry and implements, accepting the reduced effectiveness as the price of survival.

When they encountered the Aurimänner, the gold-plated horrors that haunted Astravia's sealed provinces, they discovered something remarkable. The gold integrated into these creatures resonated with their magic in ways their own implements never had. Each blast of Greschian sorcery struck the golden-touched infected with devastating effect, the metal amplifying the destructive power, the stabilization making their spells precise enough to destroy the creatures without risk of the chaotic backfire that plagued battlefield casting.

Within days of their arrival, the Greschian sorcerers had cleared three quarantine zones that the Ravii had written off as permanently lost. They strode through streets that no living Astravian had walked in generations, their blood-magic tearing through Aurimänner that had resisted everything the Ravii could bring against them. The golden components that had made these horrors so dangerous now made them vulnerable, each piece of sacred metal serving as a conduit for the sorcerers' power.

To the surviving Ravii, watching from behind their barriers as the sorcerers worked their terrible art, it seemed like salvation. These strangers from the east, refugees though they were, wielded power that could end the nightmare that had consumed their kingdom for generations. They began to speak of the Greschians as deliverers, as champions sent by the gods to cleanse what the Ravii themselves could not.

### Why Astravia Accepted Them

Three factors sealed the accord between Albrecht and Margric.

**Kinship Remembered.** The old Aurian Priests recognized the refugees' gods. Though the names had shifted over centuries and the rituals had diverged, the Veistri pantheon and the Astravian divine tradition shared the same roots, remembered from when both peoples had huddled in the same underground darkness. Both traced their ancestry to the ancient lake tribes of Gheliach, though the Ravii had forgotten that name long ago. The priests declared this not merely alliance but reunion of long-sundered kin. Religious law demanded hospitality to those who shared the old blood-bonds. To turn away fellow children of the deep would invite divine curse.

**Salvation from the Plague.** The Greschian sorcerers could do what no Astravian force had managed in generations: reclaim the lost territories. Their blood-magic, amplified by the very gold the Ravii had integrated into the infected, turned the kingdom's curse into a weapon against itself. Albrecht saw in these sorcerers the possibility of restoring Astravia to its former glory, of reclaiming the sealed provinces, of ending the Rostrot that had consumed his people for so long.

**Economic Calculation.** Beyond salvation, the refugees brought valuable knowledge. They carried Greschian grimoires containing sorcerous traditions unknown in Astravia. Their smiths knew the secrets of Sablesteel, that dark metal which held an edge longer than any Astravian iron, and they knew where it could be mined. Their administrators had governed two kingdoms and understood taxation, logistics, and organization on scales Astravia had never achieved. The Ravii had gold. The refugees had everything else a kingdom needed to thrive.

Albrecht saw opportunity in the refugees' desperation: he could save his kingdom by binding these powerful newcomers to it, expecting to profit from integration while his own house remained paramount.

He was mistaken.

### The Marriage

King Albrecht offered his only daughter, Gisela, to King Margric in marriage. The union was meant to merge bloodlines and bind the Astravian royal house to the refugee king's destiny while maintaining Astravian primacy. Margric accepted, becoming King of both peoples.

But Gisela was not merely a political token. She was young, devout, and had served as an acolyte among the Aurian Priests before her father called her back to court. When she first saw Margric demonstrate his Greschian sorcery, blood coiling through the air at his command, power radiating from him that dwarfed anything the Aurian Priests could muster, she was struck by something deeper than political duty.

Here was a king who had led his people through the demon-haunted wastes of Pyrgon's frontier. Who had faced the Colossi and survived. Who carried in his veins the blood of two royal lines and the power to command magic itself. Who had, in mere weeks, accomplished what her entire kingdom had failed to do for generations: push back the Rostrot, reclaim the sealed provinces, give her people hope.

Her admiration became fixation. Her fixation became something far more consuming.

### The Birth of Aurian Sorcery

Gisela possessed a secret she had shared with no one. Through the ancient bloodlines that connected Astravia to its Gheliach origins, she carried latent potential for blood magic. The Aurian Priests had never recognized it for what it was. They called it "divine sensitivity" and trained her to interpret omens and conduct rituals. But Gisela had felt the power stirring within her, formless and unreachable, a current she could sense but never grasp.

When she married Margric and witnessed true Greschian sorcery, she understood what lay dormant in her blood. She studied the Greschian grimoires in secret, learning the theory of their magic, recognizing in their descriptions the very sensations she had felt all her life. But she was forbidden from binding herself to Gheliach. The binding ritual required a journey to the ruined kingdom of Greschia itself, and Margric would never permit his queen to undertake such a dangerous pilgrimage. More importantly, Gisela did not want to bind her soul to a demonic realm. She wanted to serve her husband, not become beholden to powers beyond his domain.

She knew what the Aurian Priests knew: that gold could be integrated into living flesh. She knew what the Greschian sorcerers knew: that gold stabilized and amplified blood magic, made the chaotic energies of Gheliach cooperative and controllable. And she knew what neither group had ever combined: that Astravian gold, pure enough to be imbued without poisoning its bearer, might serve as a permanent conduit for the bloodline connection she possessed.

What followed was not experimentation. It was obsession given form through flesh and agony.

#### The Transformation

Gisela began in secret, in chambers beneath the palace where none would hear her screaming.

She started with gold dust mixed into wine, drinking it each night until her stomach cramped and her vomit ran glittering. The Aurian Priests had used this technique for minor blessings, small amounts to sanctify the body for ritual. Gisela consumed quantities that would have killed a lesser woman, her latent bloodline potential keeping her alive through what should have been fatal poisoning. She could feel the gold settling into her, coating the inside of her throat, her stomach, her intestines. It was not enough.

She moved to injections. Golden needles, thin as hair, pushed beneath the skin and left there. Molten gold, cooled just enough to remain liquid, forced into her veins with syringes she had commissioned from smiths who asked no questions. The pain was beyond description. Her blood fought the intrusion, clotting around the foreign metal, her heart laboring to pump through passages that were slowly filling with sacred ore. She bit through leather straps to keep from screaming. She cauterized her own injection sites with heated irons to prevent infection. She documented everything in journals written in her own blood, tracking which methods brought her closer to the power she could feel lurking just beyond her reach.

The gold had to reach her marrow. She understood this instinctively. The blood was the conduit, but the marrow was the source, where new blood was born. She had a smith craft hollow needles long enough to pierce bone. She drove them into her own hips, her shoulders, her sternum, injecting purified gold dust directly into the spaces where her body created life. The agony made her vision white. She passed out repeatedly, waking in pools of her own blood and bile, the needles still protruding from her flesh.

She should have died a hundred times. The gold should have poisoned her, clotted her heart, turned her blood to sludge. But her bloodline carried the potential of Gheliach, and that potential kept her alive through torments that would have destroyed anyone else. Her body learned to incorporate the gold rather than reject it. Her blood learned to flow around and through the metal rather than fighting it. Her marrow learned to produce blood that was as much gold as it was iron.

The process took months. She emerged from each session weaker, paler, closer to death. Her handmaidens whispered that the queen was ill, that she was wasting away from some unknown sickness. Margric grew concerned, summoning physicians who found nothing wrong, prescribing rest that Gisela ignored. She could not rest. She was so close. The power was there, she could feel it, she simply needed to give it the conduit it required.

The final integration was the worst. She had to reach her heart.

She had a smith craft a needle six inches long, thin but rigid, designed to slide between ribs. She positioned herself over a mirror so she could see what she was doing. She said a prayer to gods she no longer believed in. She pushed the needle into her own chest.

The gold entered her heart in a single burning rush. For a moment that stretched into eternity, she died. Her heart stopped, overwhelmed by the intrusion. Her vision went black. She felt herself falling, falling toward something vast and dark and hungry, something that recognized the blood in her veins and reached for her with terrible eagerness.

Then she gasped back to life, and the power was there.

It flowed through her like nothing she had ever experienced. Every drop of gold in her body sang in harmony, conducting the energies of her bloodline with perfect clarity. She could feel the connection to Gheliach, that distant realm of blood and chaos, but she was not bound to it. The gold acted as a barrier and a bridge simultaneously, allowing the power to flow through her without claiming her soul. And the power responded not to ritual or demonic pact, but to belief. To devotion. To the face that filled her thoughts even as she lay gasping on the floor in a pool of her own blood.

She thought of Margric, and the power surged.

When Gisela rose from the chamber where she had remade herself, she looked no different than before. The gold was inside her now, threaded through her blood and marrow, integrated so completely that it could not be seen. Her body had healed around the intrusions, her flesh closing over injection sites, her skin returning to its natural smoothness. The bloodline potential that had kept her alive through the transformation now kept her pristine, the gold within constantly maintaining her form in a state of perfect health. She would never scar. She would never age. She would never show any sign of what she had done to herself.

Only the power betrayed the change. And when she finally demonstrated that power, everything changed.

### The First Pontifex Maxima

Margric did not know what his wife had done until she demonstrated it before the court. She raised her hand, and golden light spilled from her palm, weaving into shields of shimmering force, flowing into Margric's personal guard and filling them with strength beyond their natural limits. The Greschian sorcerers present recognized elements of their own magic, the blood-connection to Gheliach that powered it, but twisted into something alien and new. Something that did not require binding. Something fueled by devotion rather than demonic pact.

What passed between Margric and Gisela in private afterward remains unknown. What emerged was a new order. Gisela became the Pontifex Maxima, the Greatest Bridge-Builder, she who bridged the gap between mortal and divine, between the faithful and their living god. The title deliberately echoed the old Aurian Priesthood from which she had come, honoring the tradition that had provided the foundation for her transformation.

Over the following years, Gisela shaped a faith around her husband. She trained others in the agonizing process of gold integration, selecting young women with bloodline potential, subjecting them to carefully controlled versions of what she had endured. Those who survived and could channel the magic became the first Aurian Priestesses, their title drawn from the Aurian Priests who had once served Astravia's old gods. The male priests of the old order found themselves gradually marginalized, their traditions absorbed into a new religion that venerated a living man above all divine predecessors.

Under Gisela's guidance, the Divinor Imperium took its first shape. The old gods were not yet forbidden, but they had begun to fade, overshadowed by a god-emperor whose priestesses wielded golden magic in his name.

### Why Aurian Sorcery Locks Out Other Magic

What Gisela had done was irreversible. The gold, now part of her flesh, had permanently altered her magical anatomy. The metal acted as both stabilizer and amplifier for the Gheliach energies in her blood, calming their chaos, making them controllable. But in doing so, it had filled the channels that practitioners normally use to bind themselves to magical traditions.

A Greschian sorcerer binds their soul to Gheliach through the Bounded Circle, establishing a direct connection to the Demon Pool. A practitioner of other traditions binds themselves through other pathways, other configurations of the pattern within their minds. Gisela's gold had claimed all of these channels, configuring them permanently to flow only through the sacred metal, only through the stabilizing and amplifying properties that gold provided.

She could never bind herself to Gheliach now, even if she wished to. The pathways were occupied. She could never practice any other form of bounded magic, never attune to any other circle. The gold had made those channels its own, and gold admits no rivals.

This was both limitation and protection. Aurian practitioners drew upon Gheliach through inheritance rather than pact. They owed nothing to that realm. Their souls remained their own. They could not be corrupted by demonic influence in the way Greschian sorcerers risked with their direct binding. But they could never learn other magics, never expand beyond what their devotion could fuel.

The gold had to be pure enough to avoid poisoning the body over decades. Only Astravian gold, refined to near-perfection, could serve. Lesser gold would slowly kill its bearer as impurities accumulated in the flesh. This was why Astravia, and only Astravia, could have birthed this tradition. No other land possessed gold pure enough for the imbuing to succeed without eventual death.

### Why They Became Ravanians

The question of identity plagued the merged peoples in those early years. What were they now? The Veistri had lost Valk. The Greschians had abandoned Greschia. Both names carried the weight of defeat, of failure, of homelands destroyed and people scattered. To call themselves Veistri was to remember the barbarian hordes crashing against their walls, the Colossus rising through the fog, the capital burning. To call themselves Greschians was to remember the demons of Pyrgon, the endless retreat, the homeland they could never reclaim.

But Astravia had received them. The Ravii had opened their borders, shared their gold, given their princess. This land, not the Valegren plateau or the Argyros peninsula, was where the combined people would build their future. The name Ravanian emerged naturally, honoring the Ravii who had made their survival possible while acknowledging that this new identity was no longer purely Veistri, Greschian, or Astravian.

More importantly, the new faith required a new identity. The Divinor Imperium preached that Margric was divine, that his priestesses wielded sacred power through devotion to him. This was not a Veistri belief or a Greschian belief. It was born in Astravia, from an Astravian princess using Astravian gold, shaped by Astravian religious traditions that had always held gold to be sacred. To be Ravanian was to embrace this new truth. To cling to Veistri or Greschian identity was to hold onto the past, onto gods who had failed, onto homelands that had burned.

The Ravii themselves did not resist this absorption. They were too few, too grateful for salvation, too awed by the sorcerers who had cleansed their plagued provinces. Within two generations, they had merged completely into the new identity. The word Ravanian came to mean anyone descended from the combined peoples, whether their ancestors had fled Valk, practiced sorcery in Greschia, or mined gold in Astravia.

Only the language preserved the old distinctions. Astriv, the tongue that modern Ravanians still speak, drew heavily from Astravian vocabulary and grammar, blended with Veistri military terms and Greschian magical nomenclature. The Ravii contributed the foundation upon which everything else was built.

### The Exodus to Eldvatr

Despite the Greschian sorcerers' success against the Aurimänner, Astravia could not serve as the permanent home for the combined peoples. The reasons were numerous and compelling.

The land itself was depleted. Generations of plague had consumed not just lives but resources. The sealed provinces, even after cleansing, remained tainted places where crops failed and water ran foul. The quarantine zones had been locked away for so long that reclamation would take decades of work. The infrastructure had crumbled. The roads were broken. The mines that once produced Astravian gold now sat flooded or collapsed, their workers long dead or fled. The Ravii who remained were too few to rebuild what their ancestors had made, and the refugees were too exhausted from years of flight to settle into such demanding labor.

The combined population was simply too large for what remained. Hundreds of thousands of refugees had arrived from the east, adding to the hundreds of thousands of Ravii who survived. The western heartland of Astravia, the only region that remained truly habitable, could not support such numbers. Famine loomed. The treasury, already strained by generations of plague management, could not purchase enough grain from distant lands to feed everyone through even a single winter.

Most pressingly, the threats had not ended. The Colossi still wandered the Wildvasts to the east. The demons of Pyrgon still multiplied in their ruined kingdom. Scouts reported movement along the borders, hints that whatever forces had destroyed Valk and driven the refugees westward were still active, still hungry. Astravia lay too close to those dangers. Its depleted defenses could not withstand another catastrophe.

But beyond Astravia's western marches lay Eldvatr, a vast region of fertile valleys and mineral-rich mountains inhabited only by scattered tribes of Gigamar, Demiliss, and unallied humans. The Greschian sorcerers who scouted these lands returned with promising reports. Rich soil. Clean water. And most importantly, deposits of Sablesteel, that dark metal the Veistri and Greschians had used for generations. They recognized the black-veined ore immediately. Here was everything they needed to build anew.

The decision was made. The combined peoples would not rebuild Astravia. They would conquer Eldvatr and found something new: an empire that honored their Astravian salvation while leaving behind the cursed lands of the east.

### The Conquest of Eldvatr

In the final years of the Third Era and the early years of the Fourth, the Ravanians swept through Eldvatr. Their Greschian sorcerers shattered tribal defenses with blood-magic. Their Veistri veterans, hardened by years of war against Pyrgon's demons, proved vastly superior to the scattered warriors they faced. Their Aurian Priestesses, still few in number but growing, blessed their armies and cursed their enemies.

They destroyed what they deemed abominations and monsters, even when those creatures bore human form and other bloodlines. They enslaved the Gigamar and Demiliss as they had done in their former lands. The native human tribes were given a choice: submit and be absorbed, or resist and be annihilated. Most submitted. Those who did not served as examples.

The conquest took years. The native peoples did not surrender easily, and the land itself fought back. But the Ravanians possessed advantages their enemies could not match: organization, sorcery, divine fervor, and the desperate determination of people who had already lost everything once and would not do so again.

In Eldvatr, they founded the Ravanian Empire. The golden spires of Astravian architecture rose again, but now they crowned a city built for empire rather than survival. The Divinor Imperium established its great cathedral there, and the Pontifex Maxima took her place as second only to the Kaiser himself.

**The Aftermath**
Many quarantine zones were never reopened, their contents deemed too dangerous even for Greschian sorcerers, their boundaries maintained as monuments to what the Ravii had endured. The eastern reaches of what was once Astravia remain largely empty to this day, haunted places where the golden domes still gleam over streets that no living foot has walked in centuries.

The Pontifex Maxima Gisela lived for over one hundred and seventy years, serving her divine husband across generations. She trained the first Aurian Priestesses, established the rituals of the Divinor Imperium, and codified the theological doctrines that would govern Ravanian faith. When she finally died, peacefully in her sleep as all Aurian practitioners do if not felled by war, she was buried in a tomb of solid gold beneath the great cathedral of Valcris.

Her successors would eventually forbid mention of the old Veistyrian gods who had abandoned Valk in its darkest hour. They spoke less often of the Astravian gods who had merged with the imperial faith. But the gold that empowers every Aurian priestess, the gold demanded in religious tribute, the gold that decorates every cathedral and shrine, this carries the legacy of Astravia in every gleaming surface. And every priestess who undergoes the imbuing walks a path that Gisela first walked alone, in secret, driven by nothing but devotion to a man she had decided was worthy of worship.

### The Modern Imbuing Ritual

What Gisela endured in secret agony, modern initiates experience in sacred ceremony.

Over the centuries following the founding of the Divinor Imperium, the Aurian Priestesses refined the imbuing process into something far removed from its brutal origins. Gisela had been forced to drive needles into her own flesh, to inject molten gold into her own veins, to pierce her own heart with no guidance but desperation and faith. Her successors learned from her suffering. They studied the patterns of gold integration in her body and in the bodies of the early priestesses who survived their own ordeals. They developed techniques to replicate those patterns without requiring the initiate to mutilate herself.

The modern ritual requires a circle of  fully imbued Aurian Priestesses, the number dependent on their strength, surrounding the initiate. The young woman, already tested for bloodline potential and deemed suitable, lies upon an altar of pure Astravian gold. She drinks a preparation of gold dust suspended in sanctified wine, the same beginning Gisela once used, but in carefully measured doses rather than the reckless quantities of those first experiments.

Then the priestesses begin their work.

Using their own Aurian sorcery, the priestesses channel power through the gold of the altar and into the initiate's body. The magic carries the gold dust from her stomach into her blood, from her blood into her marrow, from her marrow into every corner of her being. Where Gisela had to force the integration through violence, the priestesses guide it through power that has already mastered the pathways of gold and flesh. The initiate feels warmth spreading through her body, a pressure that builds and builds, a golden light behind her closed eyes. She feels her blood changing, her pattern reshaping, her connection to the power of her bloodline finally opening.

It takes hours. It requires the priestesses to maintain perfect concentration, their faith unwavering, their power flowing in harmony. If any of the priestesses falters, the ritual must be abandoned and attempted again another day. But when it succeeds, the initiate rises from the altar transformed, her body now threaded with gold as surely as if she had endured Gisela's torments, yet bearing no memory of pain.

The process is not without risk. Some initiates prove unable to accept the gold despite showing bloodline potential in testing. Their bodies reject the integration, and they die upon the altar despite the priestesses' best efforts. Others survive but emerge unable to channel the power, the gold within them inert and useless. These failed initiates are given honorable positions within the church as Scriba Sacra, copying sacred texts, their bodies still carrying gold that serves no magical purpose.

But for those who succeed, the imbuing is a moment of transcendence. They rise as Aurian Priestesses, their flesh pristine, their blood golden, their devotion given form and power. They join the sisterhood that Gisela founded, walking a path she first walked in blood and screaming, now made gentle through centuries of refinement and the accumulated power of all the priestesses who came before.

The journals Gisela kept during her transformation remain sealed in the deepest vaults beneath the cathedral of Valcris. Only the Pontifex Maxima may read them. The modern priestesses know that their founder suffered to create what they now inherit painlessly, but the details of that suffering are kept hidden. It is enough to know that she gave everything. The specifics would only disturb the faithful.
