Clerics are magic users whose power manifests through religious devotion and ritual practice. Their magic flows through faith in divine entities, channeled through prayers, ceremonies, and adherence to sacred doctrine. Unlike circle-bound magic, which requires binding to specific entities or locations, or Oathbound magic, which draws from personal conviction alone, cleric magic operates within religious frameworks passed down through generations of theological tradition.

What distinguishes clerics from other practitioners is the method by which they channel their potency. Where a circle-bound mage binds directly to a source, and an Oathbound caster shapes power through personal will, clerics submit themselves to religious hierarchy and sacred law. Their magic manifests in forms dictated by their faith's teachings. Prayers learned in childhood become spells. Rituals performed correctly produce consistent results. Devotion to divine principles shapes raw potency into miracles that align with theological doctrine.

Different religions produce different manifestations of cleric magic. Aurian sorcery, practiced by priestesses of the Divinor Imperium in Ravania, focuses on healing, life extension, and blessings tied to vitality and growth. Other faiths in other lands channel their devotion into different forms: war blessings, funerary rites, nature magic, divine judgment, or prophetic visions. The shape the magic takes reflects what the practitioners believe their deity or deities value and permit.

Clerics require natural potency like any other magic user. Faith does not create power from nothing, but it teaches the faithful how to channel what exists within them through religious structure. Some theologians argue this proves the divine grants power to the worthy. Others counter that belief itself is sufficient, that conviction shapes potency into forms that reflect what the practitioner expects to happen. The debate continues in seminaries and temples across the known world, unresolved and perhaps unresolvable.

What remains consistent across all cleric traditions is that their power depends on maintaining religious practice. A cleric who abandons daily prayers, violates sacred law, or strays from doctrine does not lose their power immediately or completely. Instead, their growth becomes stunted. Their potency stops expanding as it would for other practitioners. The magic they already possess remains functional, but it calcifies, unable to deepen or develop further. Return to proper observance can resume growth, though some theologians claim prolonged apostasy causes permanent damage to one's spiritual capacity.

Extremely rare exceptions exist. Heretical clerics, those who deliberately pervert their faith's teachings through sacrilegious rituals and forbidden practices, sometimes manage to continue expanding their power despite abandoning orthodoxy. They steal the forms and structures of religious magic but twist them toward blasphemous ends, channeling their potency through inverted prayers and desecrated rites. How they bypass the growth limitation that afflicts ordinary apostates remains a subject of horrified speculation. Some theorize they have simply found new gods to serve, dark reflections of the faiths they betrayed. Others believe they have discovered something fundamental about the nature of divine magic that orthodox practitioners refuse to acknowledge.

This creates a fundamental difference from Oathbound magic. When an Oathbound caster breaks their oath, the consequences are immediate and catastrophic: power loss, backlash, sometimes death. When a cleric violates their faith, the punishment is slower, subtler, a ceiling imposed on their potential rather than destruction of what they've already achieved. Some fallen clerics continue practicing their diminished magic for years, sustained by whatever power they possessed before their faith faltered.

Cleric magic is more reproducible than many other forms of magic, though it is not unique in this quality. A prayer that works for one priest will generally work for another who speaks the same words with the same devotion, assuming equal potency and faith. A ritual performed correctly tends to produce consistent results regardless of who performs it. This institutional consistency makes cleric magic valuable for organizations, armies, and governments. It can be taught, standardized, and deployed with reasonable predictability. However, bounded circle magics such as those practiced by Arcanists and Sigilists achieve even greater reproducibility. Their techniques, being bound to specific magical circles and standardized methodologies, produce results that are nearly identical regardless of the caster, limited only by potency and skill. Where cleric magic requires both proper form and proper faith, Arcanist formulas and Sigilist symbols function through pure technical precision. A sigil inscribed correctly will work the same way every time, without requiring the creator to believe in anything beyond the mechanics of its construction.

However, this structure also constrains. A cleric cannot easily improvise or adapt their magic outside what their religion permits. Innovation requires theological justification, approval from religious authorities, sometimes centuries of debate before new prayers are accepted into canon. Where a wild mage might invent entirely new applications of power, a cleric must work within established traditions or risk being declared heretical.

The relationship between clerics and other magic users varies by religion and culture. Some faiths view all non-cleric magic as profane or dangerous. Others acknowledge different paths to power while maintaining their own approach is superior or divinely ordained. Still others incorporate multiple traditions, allowing practitioners to study both religious and secular magic, though rarely with equal depth in both.

Across all variations, what defines a cleric is this: their magic is inseparable from their faith, shaped by religious tradition, and sustained through continued devotion to principles they believe are divine.
