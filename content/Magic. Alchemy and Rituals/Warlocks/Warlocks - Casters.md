Warlocks resemble wizards in many ways, both typically utilizing bounded magic. The crucial distinction lies not in their techniques but in what they bind to. Wizards bind themselves to relics that serve as pure translators of magical potency, non-thinking conduits that channel power through established patterns. Warlocks bind themselves not only to a relic but also to a monarch, an entity with will, intention, and purposes of its own. The monarch's identity usually remains unknown to the warlock, or at least incompletely understood.

What monarchs actually are remains a matter of speculation and debate. Some scholars theorize they were once mortal, practitioners who achieved such tremendous power and bound their original forms and became something else entirely. Others believe they are forces or concepts that gained awareness through unknown means. Still others suggest they may be relics themselves that developed sentience over eons of accumulated magical use. How one becomes a monarch, if such a transformation is even possible, is completely unknown. What is certain is that monarchs possess will and agency. They are not mere conduits. They choose, they act, they demand.

A relic in the context of bounded magic can be an object such as an orb, a book, or an obelisk, or it can be a place or part of an environment such as a mountain, a pool, or a stretch of forest. Each is unique and must continue to exist for the magic to function. Bounded circles serve as translators of attuned potency, anchoring magical practice to something tangible and persistent in the world. For warlocks, the relic serves a dual purpose. It functions as a bounded circle, providing the structure through which magic flows, but it is also the monarch's anchor. Whether the relic is the monarch's prison, their home, or merely a doorway through which they touch the world depends entirely on the individual monarch and the circumstances of their binding.

The process of becoming a warlock can occur through accident or deliberate choice. Some bind accidentally, touching the wrong object or drinking from the wrong source, and find themselves claimed by a monarch before they understand what has happened. Noxificers, who bind to the entity dwelling in fouled water, are a prime example of accidental binding. Others seek out monarchs deliberately, researching which relics house these entities and choosing to bind with full awareness of what they are doing. Greschian sorcerers, who bind to the Monarch of Gheliach, represent this deliberate path. The binding cannot be inherited through bloodline. Each warlock must make their own pact, whether knowingly or not.

In rare cases involving niche rituals, multiple warlocks can bind to the same monarch through different relics. This creates a strange kinship among those who serve the same entity, though whether they cooperate or compete depends on the monarch's nature and what it demands of them.

What monarchs want in exchange for granting power varies as widely as the monarchs themselves. Some seek freedom from their bindings. Others hunger for experiences they can no longer have in their transformed state, if they were ever mortal at all. Some pursue ancient grudges or incomprehensible goals. A few seem motivated by curiosity, boredom, or something approaching mentorship. Many appear as morally varied as mortals, though whether this is because they once were mortal or simply because consciousness breeds complexity is impossible to determine.

Binding to multiple circles or monarchs provides horizontal growth rather than vertical. A warlock who binds to a second monarch does not become twice as powerful. Instead, they gain access to a broader range of abilities, different tools rather than simply more strength. This makes such bindings strategically valuable but does not bypass the normal limitations of potency growth.

Physical alterations from the binding are very rare but can occur. Some warlocks develop scars, marks, or mutations that reflect their connection to the monarch. These changes are typically subtle at first but may become more pronounced with prolonged service or intense use of the monarch's power.

Breaking a warlock's pact is dangerous and poorly understood. Those who attempt to sever their connection to a monarch usually lose their potency entirely or die in the process. Whether this is the monarch's active punishment, a consequence of ripping apart the binding, or something else entirely remains unknown. The few who claim to have succeeded rarely speak of how they managed it, and their accounts are inconsistent enough to suggest either they are lying or that each unbinding requires unique and dangerous methods.

The historical origins of warlocks are lost to time. The practice first appeared during the Age of Mass Emergence, but whether warlocks were created deliberately, evolved naturally, or arose through some catastrophic accident is a matter of speculation. What is certain is that they have existed long enough to become recognized, if not always accepted, across multiple civilizations.

Warlocks cannot bind to a monarch without a relic. The relic is essential, the point of contact through which the monarch's power reaches the warlock and through which the warlock's service reaches the monarch. Without it, the connection cannot form or sustain itself.

Social perception of warlocks varies widely depending on culture and circumstance. Some view them as no different from other bounded magic users, merely practitioners who chose a relic with an inhabitant. Others fear them as servants of unknowable entities with hidden agendas. The fact that most warlocks do not fully understand their own monarchs does little to ease this suspicion. Trusting a warlock means trusting not only the individual but also the entity that grants them power, and that entity's motives are rarely transparent.
